MEWD — THE GODOT PORT
=====================

(MEWD: Made Entirely Without Doom.)

At the user's request: the whole game rewritten in Godot 4 (4.3, now
4.7), in GDScript, on the branch claude/godot-rewrite. It was ported
from the JavaScript build that was in js/, and the ported files still
say which JS file they came from in their first lines; that build has
since been deleted at the user's request (it is in the git history).
Where this file says "the web build" it means that one.


RUNNING IT

  The project is the repository's root: open the folder in Godot 4.7
  and press F5, or from a shell:

    godot                      # the editor
    godot --path . -- --play   # straight into the game, past the title

  Bake the islands once first (see THE ISLAND below; a couple of
  minutes, and again only when an island or its code changes):

    godot --headless --script res://tools/bake_island.gd -- --all --if-missing

  tools/ carries a .gdignore. The game's own assets are res://assets/.

  Arguments after `--`:
    --play               skip the title
    --seed=N             the crowd for seed N (where the townsfolk are
                         dropped on the island; the island is the same)
    --map=NAME           which island (godot/scripts/level/islands.gd):
                         island0 (NOT PENNSYLVANIA) or candyland
    --at=X,Y,DEG         stand somewhere else (for pictures), in the
                         game's units (32 to the metre)
    --eye=RISE[,PITCH]   the eye that far over the body, tilted PITCH
                         degrees (for pictures)
    --weapon=NAME        start holding FLAMER, EXTINGUISHER, BORE,
                         MINIGUN, LANCE, LAUNCHER, ARC or POTATO
    --autofire           hold the trigger (for pictures and tests)
    --shot=out.png       render --shot-frames=N frames (default 20),
                         save the picture, quit — how every picture of
                         the port was checked
    --title              with --shot, stay on the title
    --pause              open the pause menu
    --touch              the phone's controls, without a touch screen
    --zoom=N             the scope of the gun in hand starts at step N
                         (the lance's screen, the launcher's thermal)
    --prof               print where a frame's time goes, every 2 s
                         (fps, draw calls, and the game's own sections);
                         FRAME RATE in the pause menu's DEBUG tab shows
                         the same under the frame rate, on any machine

  Controls are the web build's: WASD or the arrows (Q/E strafe too),
  mouse to look (click to capture it), Shift to run, Space to jump, F
  to use, left mouse or Ctrl to fire, Z, C or the right button to put
  the lance's scope or the launcher's thermal sight up and step its zoom
  (AIM and ZOOM on the phone), 1-7 or the wheel for the guns,
  Esc or P to pause. On the title, up/down and Enter, or the mouse.
  A PAD, ANY PAD (godot/scripts/pad.gd): the one built into a handheld
  (an Anbernic RG557), a USB or Bluetooth controller, or both at once —
  every binding is for every device, not device 0, and a pad the engine
  has no mapping for is given the standard layout on arrival. Left
  stick or D-pad walks, right stick looks, R2 fires, L2 or X jumps, A
  uses, B steps the scope, the bumpers cycle the guns (Y goes forward),
  a stick pressed in runs, Start or Select pauses. In the menus the
  D-pad or a stick moves, A takes, B goes back, the bumpers turn the
  pause menu's pages; at the terminal A is Enter and B Backspace. A pad
  in use takes the phone's thumb controls off the picture; a finger on
  the glass brings them back. (On the web the browser tells the game
  about a pad only after its first press.)
  F2 in the game opens the map editor on the map last edited (a test
  run from the editor has an "◀ EDITOR F2" button too).

  SLOW MOTION (the Godot build's own, at the user's request): T, the
  right stick pressed in on the pad (Pad's "slow"; the wizard asks for
  it), or SLO on the glass, toggles it. The world runs one tic in
  four (Game.SLOW_MO) while your own body takes all four, so you walk,
  turn and fire as fast as ever through a world that crawls — bullet
  time, not a replay; the music slows with it, and the HUD says so at
  the top of the screen. Not in a network match, whose worlds must
  agree. The weapons test checks the tic count and the player's draw.

  BRAINS (game/trophies.gd; at the user's request, "let the player
  collect body parts extracted with the cerebral bore"): when a bore
  finishes its drilling and the head goes, what it was after drops out
  — a brain, out of the gore strip, which falls to the floor and lies
  there turning until a player walks over it and takes it. A trophy
  and nothing more: BRAINS n on the HUD under the gun's name
  (Player.brains), a toast as each is taken. The weapons test finds
  the one its bore kill left and walks over it.


THE ISLAND: NO MORE MAPS, NO MORE EDITOR

  At the user's request the game left the Doom-like world behind: no
  map editor, no sectors and lines, no hand-made maps. The player, the
  crowd, the guns and everything the guns do stay. The ground under them
  is now golf's procedural island (the user's other project,
  verdictzero/golf): island_0, with golf taken out of it, and the user
  dropped on it with a few hundred townsfolk. The aim, in the user's
  words, is levels fully procedurally generated from assets they
  provide, to spare the artist and make it more fun.

  The user chose how islands are made: each one is generated ahead of
  time and BAKED, and the game ships a set of them behind a level
  select (NEW GAME on the title lists them). Island 0 is the first.
  Responders are off for now; there is just a big crowd.

  WHAT CAME FROM GOLF (godot/island/, the same file names as golf's,
  every res:// path moved under godot/island/): the island field (the
  world as pure functions of x and z: SCRIPT_island_field.gd), the chunk
  mesher, the streaming world, the bake stores and their two autoloads
  (TerrainBake, VegBake), the forest and grass scatters, the sky cycle
  with golf's "blood night" preset held at day, the cloud sea under the
  island and the horizon clouds, their shaders, materials and textures,
  and the plant sprites. About 7 MB. island_0's scene was copied and cut
  down to those: no golf course, no golf wall or forcefield, no crash
  site, no escape pod, no ruins, no glitch blobs, no nightmare sky, no
  fly camera, no golf loading screen (this game's covers the build) and
  no physics bodies (nothing here uses Godot physics). The field is
  ISLANDFIELD_island0.tres: golf's hub_solid with five switches off —
  zone_enabled (holes, fairways, build pads), sand_enabled, crater_enabled,
  divot_enabled and ruin_enabled — so the hills, forest, coast and cliffs
  are golf's island's and nothing golf carved into the ground is there.
  golf's shader globals (tod_*, sea_*, veg_*, golf_wall_*) are declared
  in project.godot; the golf_wall ones stay at zero, so the wall the
  shaders know how to draw is never drawn.

  THE ENGINE WENT TO 4.7 FIRST, on its own commit, because golf is a 4.7
  project: the project converted with nothing but a .uid beside each
  script, every suite passed, and CI built the apk and the Linux build on
  4.7 before anything else changed. The renderer is still Mobile on
  Vulkan (golf's is Compatibility); golf's island draws the same under
  it.

  ONE WORLD, TWO SCALES. The game counts 32 units to the metre (a person
  is 56 tall) and the island counts metres. Rather than renumber either,
  the Game node is scaled to 1/32 and the island, under it, back up by
  32: both draw in one world at the true size, the island's fog and fade
  distances (all metres) mean what golf meant by them, and the game's
  simulation never sees the difference. What did notice: anything that
  read a camera's GLOBAL position or axes against the game's own
  positions (the bore's and the launcher's reticles, the tracers, the arc
  gun's bolts, the thermal sight's brackets, the muzzle on the glass) now
  reads the camera's local ones; the camera's near and far planes are in
  metres; and the shaders that build a sprite out of sizes in the game's
  units (the particles, the flame, the crowd's lean and bob, the blast
  and sear decals' depth test) multiply by a new global, game_unit, the
  metres in a unit (U.set_unit: 1/32 in a game, 1 on the title). The
  lance's light on the world is handed to the shaders in metres too.

  THE LEVEL IS STILL A LEVEL (godot/scripts/level/island_level.gd). The
  game only ever asked its level a few things — how high is the floor
  here, may a body move from here to there, can an eye see a point,
  where does a ray stop — so IslandLevel extends Level and answers them
  off the ground: every point on the island is a sector of its own, open
  to the sky, its floor the ground under it and its ceiling far
  overhead. There are no lines and no walls. A body may not step more
  than Doom's 24 units up in one move (so a crag is a wall), the crowd
  may not drop more than 96, and nobody walks within two metres of the
  coast (the cliff's lip; past it is cloud). An eye is checked against
  the ground every three metres along its line, so a hill hides what is
  behind it and a hummock does not; a ray is walked a metre at a time and
  halved down to where it goes into the ground. It says it is `layered`,
  so every caller that used to ask room-over-room's questions (rounds
  stopped by decks, blasts not through floors, the crowd and you only
  bumping at your own height, sound with height in it) asks the ground
  instead. A round fired level into a hillside used to fly through it —
  the level shots never asked the floor — and now stops in it, and a
  hole in the ground faces out of the slope it is in.

  THE GROUND AS A GRID (godot/scripts/level/island_ground.gd). Asking
  the field where the ground is costs 34 microseconds a point, far too
  dear for every body and every drop of blood every tic. So it is asked
  once, on a 2 m grid at exactly the points the terrain mesh's finest
  LOD has its corners on (1691 x 1691 of them, every core at once), and
  the game reads between them in a microsecond. Against the field at
  random points it is within 3 cm — the same ground the eye sees.

  THE BAKE (tools/bake_island.gd). golf's lesson, kept: the island takes
  minutes to build from nothing on a handheld (golf measured two and a
  half), so it is built ahead of time. The tool builds the island the way
  the game would without one, with golf's stores recording, and writes
  the terrain's chunk meshes (93 MB), the plants (11 MB) and the ground
  grid (5 MB) under signatures of everything they are a function of — the
  field's settings and the code that makes them — plus CODE_DIGESTS.json,
  the sources' digests, which an exported build needs to find its bakes
  by (its scripts are compiled, and hash to nothing: golf shipped a build
  once that carried its bake and built the island anyway, every launch,
  for want of this). The bakes go in res://godot/island/data/bake, which
  git ignores; tools/build-linux.sh and tools/build-android.sh bake any
  island whose bake is missing before they export, the exports carry
  *.bake, and CI keeps the bakes between runs, made again only when the
  island or its code changes. Checked on an exported build: it reads all
  three off disk and builds nothing. Without a bake the game still runs;
  it samples the ground at load (a minute here) and meshes the island
  behind the loading screen.

  LOADING. NEW GAME's loading screen now stays up until the island is
  raised — its terrain off the bake and its plants planted — and a --shot
  counts its frames from there. Off the bake on this machine the game is
  built in about 2.5 s and the island up a few seconds later.

  WHO IS ON IT. IslandLevel.populate drops you on open, gentle ground
  near the middle and 360 townsfolk round the island in eight groups, one
  of them round you, every one on land and nothing on a crag; you start
  facing the nearest of them. The crowd chases and flees straight across
  open ground: the room-to-room planner and the doors went with the maps.
  The --seed is the crowd's; the island is always the same.

  WHAT WENT. The map editor (godot/scripts/editor/, 11,000 lines), the
  maps (the maze, JESSE, THE SPRAWL, THE GRID, THE ANNEXE), the block
  world and the compilers (DocCompile, BlockCompile, MapGeo, Earcut, the
  scatter), the doors and the room planner, the terminal's JESSE and EDIT,
  the title's MAP EDITOR and F2, and the tests that were about any of
  those. The sections further down on the editor, the maps, blocks and
  doors are left as the record of what they were. The real decals (one
  Decal node per mark, eight a tile of the level's own meshes) are off:
  the island's terrain is not cut to their tiles, so every mark is the
  quad kind for now.

  NETWORK PLAY still works and suits it: a match names its island and its
  seed, every machine has the island baked, and the host and a headless
  bot walk the ground without drawing any of it.

  TESTS (tools/godot-test.sh): weapons, island, net, death and pad. The
  island suite (godot/tests/island_test.gd) checks the ground against the
  field, you and the crowd stood on it, walking up and down hills with
  your feet on the ground, the coast stopping you, a hill hiding a point,
  a round fired down stopping in the ground, a blast hurting who is near,
  the island coming up off the bake, and the crowd still on the ground
  after ten seconds of wandering. godot/tests/island_shot.gd takes a
  picture of a gun held on a row of people on the island.

  THE VEGETATION IS VISUAL COVER, at the user's request, and stays so:
  the firs, the bushes, the ferns and the grass stop nobody and nothing
  — not you, not the crowd, not a round — exactly as in golf, where a
  tree you walk into dissolves as you reach it. Only the ground stops
  anything.

  NOT DONE YET, and known: the island's terrain takes no real decals;
  responders (police, army, vans) are off; and the set of islands is
  one.


HOW IT IS BUILT, AND WHERE EACH PIECE OF THE WEB BUILD WENT

  THE PICTURE (js/lofi.js -> godot/scripts/render/lofi.gd,
  godot/shaders/lofi.gdshader). The world is drawn into a SubViewport
  as many pixels across as the chunky PIXELS grid has columns (320 rows
  of 2:3 pixels; RENDER in the pause menu can ask for more), and the
  filter runs ONCE PER CHUNKY PIXEL into a grid-sized buffer — it
  Bayer-dithers one step and snaps to the earth palette through the
  same 32x32x32 atlas the web build builds (godot/data/palette_lut.png)
  — which is blown up to the window nearest-neighbour: a 4K window costs
  what a small one does. Every texture is sampled nearest, every
  surface is unlit (its light is worked out in its own shader), and the
  renderer is MOBILE on the desktop and Android, at the user's request
  for real decals, and Compatibility on the web (Godot's rule). The
  picture is the same under both, to within the dither: see THE TWO
  RENDERERS. The gun is a second
  SubViewport in a world of its own, composited over the room and under
  the filter, exactly as the web build's overlay pass. The HUD, the
  title's menu and the pause menu are CanvasLayers above all of that and
  are never filtered.

  THE TWO RENDERERS. The look was tuned under Compatibility, which
  converts no colour anywhere: a texture is read as its bytes, a colour
  handed to a shader arrives as it was given, and a 3D viewport holds
  what the shader wrote. Mobile does all three differently — it decodes
  a texture or uniform marked source_color, decodes ANY Color handed to
  a material's uniform whatever its hint, and sRGB-encodes a 3D
  viewport's picture on the way out — and the same scene came out paler
  in every dim or fogged surface and the sky. So, for both to draw the
  same: no shader of the game's marks anything source_color (Compat-
  ibility ignores the hint anyway); every material uniform is set
  through U.col, which under Mobile encodes a Color first so the decode
  lands on the value meant (the global uniforms are left alone by both
  renderers, and go through U.gset, which keeps a copy — Mobile will
  not read one back); and every 3D viewport whose picture is read as
  numbers (the world, the gun, the scopes' feeds, the editor's 3D view)
  is HDR under Mobile (U.raw_out), which is what stops the encode.
  And the gun is not laid over the room by its alpha under Mobile: a
  Mobile 3D target keeps two bits of alpha, and on a phone's Adreno
  whole pieces of a solid gun came out as holes. The room's picture is
  drawn in the gun's own world instead, a quad at the far plane
  (gun_room.gdshader; Lofi.gun_on_room), and the gun onto it by the
  depth test; the filter reads that one picture.

  REAL DECALS (render/real_decals.gd; Mobile only). Blood — spatters,
  pools, the older splash — scorches, the flamer's hot spots and the
  lance's sears and slag are Godot Decal nodes: boxes that paint any
  surface inside them, so a spray wraps the corner it hit, a pool runs
  over a step and a sear crosses from the wall to the floor. Bullet
  holes stay the quads they were, for the reason below. The look is
  the quads' own shaders', baked at load: each kind drawn by its shader
  in a bake mode (lit at one, no air or depth test) as colour and as
  cover — Mobile keeps two bits of alpha in a 3D target, so the cover
  travels as a colour and a 2D pass joins them — into an atlas, six
  variants a kind. What the shaders did over time is done to the nodes:
  a pool's size spreads, blood's tint dries, a hot spot's strength is
  its cover, and a sear is three decals — the scar, its heat and its
  embers, the two glows fading at their own rates. A Decal paints an
  unshaded surface with its own colour, so each mark's modulate is the
  world's light where it is (world_band, off the copied globals), and
  it fades into the air with distance. The thermal feed leaves them out.
  EIGHT A MESH: the Mobile renderer draws at most eight decals on one
  mesh, and the world was one mesh a texture. So with real decals on,
  MapGeo cuts each texture's surfaces into 512-unit tiles (MapGeo.tile;
  triangles across an edge are clipped, their UVs and colours with
  them), and RealDecals keeps count: a mark that would be a ninth on
  any tile mesh under it is refused, and laid as the quad it always was
  — nothing is lost, and nothing is dropped by the renderer unseen. A
  minigun puts dozens of holes in one spot, which is why holes stay
  quads. ONLY THE BUILT AREA IS TILED (MapGeo.tile_area, Level.bounds):
  a map in blocks stands on a ground plane 131,072 units across, and
  tiled whole that was 65,536 meshes on every map — a quarter of a
  minute to load and fifteen hundred draw calls looking at an empty
  field; past the built area each texture is one mesh that takes no
  marks (a mark that lands on no tile is a quad). AND A BIG MAP'S TILES
  ARE BIGGER (RealDecals.tile_for): 512, doubled until the built area
  is at most 400 tiles — THE SPRAWL and JESSE in 1,024s — because every
  texture in every tile is a mesh and a draw call, and a road runs
  through nearly every tile of the city: fewer meshes, and fewer real
  marks before the rest are quads. Every picture a mark can wear is
  held in the renderer's decal atlas from load (a hidden decal wearing
  each, _keep_in_atlas): the atlas is rebuilt whenever a picture comes
  in or goes out, which was a hitch the first time each variant was
  worn. Under the loading screen a mark of every kind is laid on the
  floor ahead for the warm-up's frames (RealDecals.warm_begin, Main),
  so their pipelines are built before the first rocket.
  WHAT IT COSTS NOW (godot/tests/perf_bench.gd: the real game on a map,
  its draw calls looking four ways, and two rockets into a ring of
  shoppers and a cerebral bore, timed; xvfb, the Mobile renderer):
                    level meshes   draw calls   load
    THE ANNEXE        66,079 ->57   1,400 -> 89   13.7 s -> 0.3 s
    THE MAZE          66,372 ->522  1,700 -> 556  16.8 s -> 0.6 s
    THE SPRAWL        69,719 ->1,983 2,700 -> 813  22.7 s -> 4.9 s

  THE LIGHT (js/material.js -> godot/shaders/world_light.gdshaderinc).
  One set of GLOBAL shader uniforms — sky_light, light_falloff,
  min_light, global_light, air_near, air_far, air_color — declared in
  project.godot and written by the weather (godot/scripts/game/
  weather.gd), read by every surface: walls and floors, sprites, decals,
  particles. worldBand is ported line for line: depth diminishing, the
  sky's lift, Doom's 32 light steps, then the air.

  THE LEVEL (js/level.js, js/mapgeo.js, js/editor/doc.js compileDoc ->
  godot/scripts/level/). A document (the maps write one) is compiled to
  a Level of sectors and lines with a 128-unit blockmap; the collision,
  sight and hitscan are the web build's 2.5D ones — slide along the
  lines, step 24, never a physics engine — so it moves like the web
  build and like Doom. The compiler (doc_compile.gd) is compileCore:
  edges split at T-junctions, sectors inside sectors bridged in as
  holes (rooms sharing a wall one hole), free-standing linedefs stood
  up as walls, the line overrides (blocking, blind, a skin per face,
  offsets, scale, a fill in an opening), the outside wall where a roofed
  room meets the open air, no sky walls and a roof over every roofed
  room, Doom 64's sector colours and each sector's fog, the free boxes
  (props — drawn, and solid to nothing, as on the web), the plants, and
  the scatters (scatter.gd, dart for dart the web build's). MapGeo
  builds the floors (earcut.gd round holes), ceilings, roofs, walls
  whose textures follow the world (v is height, u the way round the
  room's ring: no slots, no pegs, no aligning — at the user's request),
  the middles standing in openings and the boxes,
  one mesh per texture; a level with colours, fog or two-faced walls is
  drawn with godot/shaders/world_tint.gdshader (the colour and fog per
  vertex, faces culled).

  ROOM OVER ROOM (js/level.js's COLUMNS, js/editor/doc.js's LAYERS). A
  map drawn in the editor's layers is compiled WHOLE, as the web build
  compiles it: each layer's linedefs stood up on their own, every
  layer's outlines laid over each other (DocCompile.overlay — cut where
  they cross, walked face by face), and each face built as a COLUMN,
  Level.add_column: the room of every layer over it, bottom-up, each
  storey's ceiling brought down to the underside of the DECK under the
  one over it. A deck is Level.DECK (16) thick, always — the web
  build's have none: the slab between is solid to movers, rays, eyes
  and fire, and its edge is drawn as a band (in a building's outside
  wall where the line is one); an upper floor with no room for a deck
  over the floor under it is left out and said. The room under has the
  upper floor's texture for its ceiling. A ROOF is as thick, over the
  ceiling of the room it roofs, and its edge is drawn wherever what is
  beside it is lower (MapGeo._roof_edge) — on every map with roofs.

  A BUILDING'S OUTSIDE WALL is DocCompile.WALL_THICK (16) thick, always
  (DocCompile.thick_walls; the web build's are a face with no depth):
  a roofed room drawn against the open air is its INSIDE, and the wall
  grows out of it — a strip out of each edge and a mitred corner out of
  each outward corner, each cut to the open-air sector it stands in and
  off every room, solid sector and wall already there. A wall is solid
  from the ground to the room's ceiling, the room's roof over it; on an
  upper layer, from the room's floor (__base) over whatever is under.
  A doorway (a line marked an opening) is a PASSAGE through the wall in
  the room's own floor and roof. Where a wall meets the edge of the open
  air it stands in, the open air is cut back round it. Overrides on the
  room's lines follow: the room's side (its middle becomes the wall's
  bottom, the face it sees) and the open air's side onto the wall's
  outer face. The tests that hold a build to the web build's turn it
  off (DocCompile.thick) for that comparison. Every other sector is a
  column of one — every sector of every map not in layers — and for
  those nothing below is asked or costs anything different (the level's
  `layered` is false and each new question is behind it or behind a
  column's `above`/a line's `multi`).
    - A point is in one column: sector_at answers with its GROUND storey
      (the web build's sectorAt). The storey is asked by height:
      span_in / span_at (the one whose floor..ceiling holds z, or the
      highest under it — the web build's spanIn), and stand_in for a
      body on its feet: the highest floor a step (24) or less over them,
      so the top of a stair a step under an upper floor walks onto it.
    - A line joins two whole columns (front_col, back_col). Walking
      through it asks the two storeys at the mover's height (a hall's
      door is a door, the landing over it a wall); the crowd will not
      walk off a drop of more than 96 to the LOWER floor; nobody goes
      under a deck head first. It is drawn as its BANDS — every interval
      of z where exactly one side is open, in the skin of the storey
      that is shut there — and a middle in each HOLE, where both are.
    - A building's outside wall (a roofed room against open air) is
      walled storey by storey: only in the openings where that is so
      (mid_z — a terrace beside an office is open), each storey in its
      own room's walls; a line marked as a doorway (opening) is one on
      ITS OWN LAYER only, so a ground-floor door is not a hole in the
      wall upstairs.
    - Rays: sight_blocked and ray_hit_wall ask the storeys at the
      crossing's height, and on a layered map ray_hit_flat walks the
      segment column by column for the first floor, deck or roof it
      passes through (a sky ceiling is air). So the hitscan, the eye's
      trace and sight stop at a floor, and a round can pass over an
      upper storey's edge or under it.
    - Everything that moves or lands asks the storey by height: the
      player (stand_in on his feet, span_in in the air), the crowd and
      the troops (spawned on their layer's floor, kept on their storey,
      solid only to what is at their height), the flame, frost, water,
      bore, missiles, arc, beam, embers, giblets, rain (onto the top of
      a column) and the decals under them; a blast does not reach
      through a floor. The fire grid's planes are the storeys (as in
      js/fire.js) and ignite/douse/heat_at take the height, so a fire on
      a terrace burns up there and does not come down, and one under it
      does not go up (open air under a deck has no way up; an indoor
      room climbs slowly into the storey over it, the web build's
      CEIL_SPREAD). A client puts each body in the storey at the height
      the host's snapshot gives (the wire is unchanged); sound falls off
      with height too.
    - Vehicles, the wood, the scatters, a match's
      spawn pads and the plants stay on the ground (as in the web build).

  THE MAPS (js/maps/ -> godot/scripts/maps/) ARE ALL IN BLOCKS now (at
  the user's request: "remake the rest of the levels as well") — each
  generator builds a BlockDoc (block_doc.gd: welded vertices, a block
  from a ring or a rectangle, a line override, a thing, a scatter, the
  storeys over the ground, and the tile grid the maze and JESSE are
  laid out on merged into rectangles of one kind and made blocks) that
  BlockCompile builds. THE MAZE (maze.gd): the ground is the dirt path,

NETWORK PLAY (js/net/ -> godot/scripts/net/)

  The web build's LAN play, ported file for file, and ON THE SAME WIRE:
  the same seventeen-byte TicCmd in a binary WebSocket frame, the same
  JSON handshake, snapshots, pings and goodbyes in text frames, at
  ws://host:port/net. So a Godot client can join the Node host, a web
  page can join the Godot host, and either kind of client can play the
  other on either host.

    godot --headless --path . -- --server[=PORT]      host (default 7777)
          [--map=jesse|maze] [--seed=N] [--max=16] [--frags=N]
    godot --path . -- --join=HOST[:PORT]               join a host
          [--name=NAME]
    node tools/server.mjs --map maze --port 7777       the web build's host
    http://…/?join=HOST:PORT                           a web page joining

  or JOIN / JOIN host:port at the terminal (a failed join comes back to
  it saying why). JESSE is team deathmatch (A in SWAT black, B in army
  green), THE MAZE deathmatch with its crowd left at home; the network
  loadout is the minigun, and the match's rules (frag limit, respawns,
  spawn guard, the pvp scale, infinite ammo) are js/net/match.js's.
  TAB shows the table; the score line is at the top of the screen.

    net/ticcmd.gd     js/net/ticcmd.js — the command; rounded with
                      JavaScript's Math.round, so the bytes are the JS's
    net/protocol.gd   js/net/protocol.js
    net/transport.gd  js/net/transport.js + tools/server.mjs's socket:
                      a loopback pipe, WebSocketPeer, and a TCPServer
                      listener handing each upgraded line to the host
    net/session.gd    js/net/session.js (Local, Host)
    net/match.gd      js/net/match.js (NetMatch: DM/TDM, spawns, frags)
    net/server.gd     js/net/server.js (SimServer: handshake, the
                      rewind, snapshots)
    net/client.gd     js/net/client.js (NetClient)
    net/remote.gd     js/net/remote.js (NetGame: prediction and
                      reconciliation, the puppets drawn in the past,
                      toasts, the score board — drawn by render/hud.gd)
    net/host.gd       tools/server.mjs (the dedicated server's loop)

  The Game is many-player the way js/game.js is: game.players, each
  with a session (the game's own LocalSession for the one on this
  machine), game.rules (a NetMatch on a host), game.rewind, targets and
  collision among players, a match's own idea of a death. On a client
  the look taken each frame is held (game.net_look) and rounded into the
  tic's command, so the host turns you exactly as your screen did.

  Test hooks: --netbot (turn toward the nearest of the others through
  the command, walk at them, fire when in sight; --netbot=look holds
  fire), --quit-after=S, --report=PATH (a client's numbers), and on the
  host --spawns=x,y;x,y… (a deathmatch's pads) and --score-file=PATH.


A MAP IN BLOCKS (godot/scripts/level/block_compile.gd; at the user's
request: "layer 0 is an infinite plane the player could hypothetically
walk on forever; everything else is built on top of that ... treat it
all as geometric extrusion with texturing instead of how doom treats
stuff")

  THE EDITOR'S MAPS ARE NOT DOOM'S. The world is an infinite GROUND
  PLANE (a texture and the light of the open air), and a map is what is
  pulled up out of it: BLOCKS — a ring of vertices, a HEIGHT, and three
  textures, its SIDES, its TOP (what you walk on) and, where it floats,
  its UNDERSIDE (a ceiling) — and the light of the space under it. No
  room has a floor and a ceiling of its own; there are no upper and
  lower textures (a face is one picture from a block's base to its top,
  round every corner); there is no void. A building is wall blocks and
  a slab over them; a step is a low block; a pit is a block pushed DOWN
  (a negative height) into what it stands on. A block drawn inside
  another stands on it; a block on a higher LAYER stands on whatever the
  layers under it put there (the highest top under its footprint, so a
  slab over uneven ground floats over the low parts); a block given a
  BASE floats at it, and the air under it is a room. The document
  ("mewd-blocks": ground, vertices, blocks, lines, things, scatters,
  textures, world, layers) is EdDoc's; the old editor's files (rooms
  with floors and ceilings) are refused, and say why.

  HOW IT IS BUILT (BlockCompile.compile): every layer's rings, and the
  ground (a square GROUND_EXTENT = 65536 each way, as the outermost ring
  of layer 0), laid over each other (DocCompile.overlay); over each
  face of the plan the blocks round it, bottom-up (layer by layer, and
  on one layer the outermost first), raise the floor — each one's solid
  from its base to its top, an open STOREY wherever a base is over the
  floor it had, a storey of no height at every join of solid on solid
  (so each block's edge is a band of its own, in its own sides), and
  one more over the last top, under the sky (SKY_H = 8192). Those are
  Level.add_column's storeys, and the Level does the rest as for any
  map in layers: the solid between two storeys is a deck as thick as
  the block, its edge drawn in the block's sides (assign_bands: a
  band's texture is the storey over it's lower texture, which is the
  block's side), a floating block's underside the ceiling of the storey
  under it (lit by the block's light), its top the floor of the one
  over. Each storey knows its block (Sector.block, .over, .layer).
  Things and doors stand on the storey their layer leaves (stand_in:
  the highest floor among those a block of their layer or under left).
  The built area (Level.bounds_hint) is what the blockmap, the forest
  and the plants keep to; the ground runs on past it. Line overrides on
  an edge: `tex` (a skin on every face of the edge), `midTex` (a fill
  across it: a fence, a window), offsets and scale, blocking, and
  `door` {h, tex, style, auto, locked} — the slab across the edge from
  the floor to the ceiling of the storeys either side on its layer,
  swinging into the side that is a room (not the passage under the
  lintel block the edge belongs to), the lintel block's thickness its
  wall. A block's `air` (a map's, not the editor's) lights the open sky
  over its top where a district is dimmer than the ground; a pit (a
  block pushed down) draws its walls in its own sides (a skin put on
  its edges), and cuts away the joins it goes down through. Blocks
  floating at a base of their own are built bottom-up by base after
  the ones standing on what is under them (a panel over a panel, a
  roof on its posts), and one of them hidden inside another is no
  problem — only a block standing on the ground with its top under
  what it stands on is. Every level the game plays is a map in blocks
  (THE MAPS above); THE ANNEXE (godot/scripts/maps/layers.gd: a shop of
  a floor patch and four wall blocks with a lintel over its door, seven
  stair blocks, the office's floor a slab on the shop's walls with the
  office's walls on it and a terrace floating beside it, a roof slab on
  the layer over) was the first, and the layers test plays it. The plan
  overlay (DocCompile.overlay) hashes its segments into a grid so each
  asks only its neighbours, and a face walk that comes back through a
  vertex (two blocks meeting at a corner) is split there into the loops
  either side; JESSE's 750 blocks build in a second, THE SPRAWL's
  thousand in two and a half.


THE MAP EDITOR (godot/scripts/editor/)

  MEWD Editor, in the game: MAP EDITOR on the title (whose "Back to
  the title" comes back to it), the terminal's E or EDIT, `-- --edit`,
  or F2 from the game. An editor in the mould of SLADE and Ultimate
  Doom Builder — the plan (view2d.gd), the 3D view drawn by the game's
  own MapGeo (view3d.gd), the combined workspace (3D big and the plan
  inset, Tab swaps them; Split, 2D, 3D), the top bar, the tabs down the
  left (textures, things, scatter, the map), the inspector down the
  right, the status line and info bar; Q for visual mode; undo and
  redo; the keyboard (the Help menu) — but ON A WORLD OF BLOCKS:

    DRAWING    drag on the bare ground (Blocks mode, S) or drag a shape
               out (R: rectangle, ellipse, circle, polygon, star…) or
               click the corners out (D), and the shape stands up as
               high as the toolbar's PULL (a block drawn on a block
               stands on it, in its textures); below zero it is pushed
               down into what it is drawn on. The wheel over a block in
               3D raises it (Alt: its base), PgUp/PgDn too (Shift: the
               base); Ctrl+wheel its light (over bare ground, the
               daylight). The plan shades blocks by their top's height
               (K: by brightness, by height).
    THE ROOM   with the toolbar's Make set to ROOM, a drawn footprint
               becomes a building: a floor patch inside, a wall block
               WALL_T = 16 thick along each edge as high as the Pull,
               and a roof slab over the whole footprint on the layer
               above, standing on the walls, its underside the ceiling.
    DOORS      Doors mode (O): click the side of a wall block, in the
               plan or in 3D, and a doorway is cut through it there —
               the block cut across into three, the piece under the
               click the tool's width lifted to a LINTEL floating at
               the door's height, the door on its edge; the next click
               puts in another; Shift+click takes a door out (the
               doorway stays). The inspector's panel on top sets the
               width, height, swinging or sliding, by itself or on the
               use key, the texture (DR1_01, the pack's red-eye door,
               unless you pick another). Each face of a door is lit by
               where it looks (MapGeo.door_nodes): the side toward its
               room by the room, the other by what lies past the wall —
               a front door seen from the street is lit as the street
               is. It used to wear the room behind it on both faces,
               and the old default, the near-black DOOR0001 shutter,
               made that read as a black slab.
    THE SIDES  a texture clicked with a block selected is its Sides;
               Alt+click a side (3D) or a line (plan), or double-click a
               line, or the inspector's "Select its sides", selects
               every side of the block and paints them all; a plain
               click on one side in 3D paints that face alone (the
               line's skin, which covers the block's sides there). The
               inspector: Height, Base (on what is under, or floating
               at a height), the light under it, Top, Sides, Underside
               (when it floats); a line's skin, a fill across it, its
               offsets and scale, its door; the ground's texture and
               daylight in the Map tab, or click the ground in 3D.
    STEPS      the inspector's step generator (ed_steps.gd): a block
               between two heights cut across into strips, each a step
               higher (Stairs); or into rings, each standing in the
               last, up to a mound or down to a caldera (Rings).
    LAYERS     Alt+PgUp / Alt+PgDn, as before: the storeys. A block on
               layer 1 stands on the top of what the layers under put
               there; a click on a block of another layer in 3D goes to
               that layer. The other layers are ghosted on the plan.
    THE PANE   the Layers tab (ed_layers.gd), Photoshop's way, at the
               user's request: the stack, top storey first, a thumbnail
               of each layer's plan, its name (double-click to rename;
               doc.layerNames) and an eye that hides it from the plan's
               ghosts and the 3D view's level (never the one being
               edited; Alt+click: that one alone); opened, a layer's
               hierarchy — its blocks, a block drawn inside another
               under it, every thing under the block it stands on —
               and a click there selects on the map (and the other way
               round). Drag a layer up or down the stack (it swaps
               places with the ones it passes, each layer's floating
               blocks taking the other's height); + New, Dup (a copy on
               the first empty layer over, its floating blocks a storey
               up), ▲ ▼, Delete, the right-click menu. Each one undo.
    STYLES     BLOCK STYLES, at the user's request (room styles, before
               the blocks): a named top, sides, underside and light
               (doc.styles) painted onto blocks from the inspector's
               "Wears", or made from a block ("Save as"); change one in
               the Map tab and every block wearing it changes; give a
               block its own texture and it is off the style. The values
               are written into the blocks: the game never sees a style.
    DROP       a texture dragged out of the browser onto either view
               lands on what is under it: a block's top (Shift: its
               underside), one side (the line's skin), or the ground.
    PLAY FROM HERE  with the 3D view on screen, F5 starts the game where
               its camera stands, looking the way it looks, on the layer
               being edited (the START is moved in the copy the game
               gets, never in the map); with only the plan, or the
               camera over nothing, the START as drawn.
    PICKING    the 3D view picks against the built level — the storeys'
               floors and ceilings, the lines' bands — so what you
               click is what is drawn, read back to its block, the
               ground or its line.
    GONE       floors and ceilings, inside and outside, upper and lower
               textures, Doom 64's colours and fog per room, free props
               and linedefs (a block does both), texture alignment.
               UI scale, the scatters, the texture editor, copy and
               paste, PLAY (F5, from where the 3D camera stands) and F2
               back are as they were.

    ed_doc.gd       the document: ground, blocks, lines, things, undo,
                    the tidy after an edit, problems, layers, files
    ed_ops.gd       the edits on it (a vertex splitting an edge, a
                    block cut out of the one it is drawn in, splits,
                    merges, moves, the shapes)
    editor.gd       the state, the edits a person makes (add_block,
                    add_room, place_door, nudge_height, loop_select,
                    apply_texture...), files, keyboard
    ed_ui.gd, ed_panels.gd, ed_style.gd   the bars, panels and look
    view2d.gd, view3d.gd                  the two views
    ed_steps.gd, ed_scatter.gd            the steps, the scatters
    ed_texcompose.gd, ed_texeditor.gd     the map's own textures
    ed_script.gd    a script of edits, for pictures (--edit-ops)

  UI SCALE (at the user's request): View > UI scale, or Ctrl+= and
  Ctrl+- to step it (75% to 250%), Ctrl+0 back to 100%: the whole
  editor — bars, panels, fields, and the views with them — drawn that
  much bigger, as the window's content scale while the editor is up
  (MewdEditor.UI_SCALES); remembered in prefs.json with the grid.

  FILES: File > Open… and Save as file… (any path on the desktop; the
  file dialog), Ctrl+S into user://mewd-editor/maps/, and an autosave
  (user://mewd-editor/autosave.gssmap.json) the editor opens on; its
  grid, snap, UI scale and left panel are remembered in
  user://mewd-editor/prefs.json. File > Open: THE ANNEXE (a building in
  three layers), THE GRID, THE MAZE and JESSE (seed 7 of each) and THE
  SPRAWL — every level the game plays, each a map in blocks to pick
  apart. The compile runs in a thread, on a copy, so a big map never

  NOT HERE: the web build's editor and its files (the Godot editor's

THE IRISH POTATO CANNON (game/potatoes.gd; the Godot build's own)

  Slot 8, at the user's request: "it will shoot potatoes that bounce
  around until they hit something, with a thick rainbow particle trail.
  when they hit something the explosion will be a spectacular color
  cycling nuke equivalent". The model is the user's
  (assets/models/potato_cannon.glb — its textures brought down to 512 on
  the long side, 24 MB to 1.6, and the barrel's knotwork UVs, which the
  file had collapsed to streaks, laid round it again), held the way the
  file draws it — the sight at the back by the eye, the hopper of
  potatoes (its glass kept glass) out front — its little screen
  a THERMAL SIGHT in night-vision green (ThermalScope with `green`: the
  launcher's heat picture run up a green ramp, a plain cross with lob
  ticks, the range in metres, six potato pips, the zoom; Z raises the
  gun so the screen is straight ahead, the hold solved from where the
  screen is in the model), and every
  other part in its OWN material — chrome, gold, red lacquer, the
  knotwork's normal maps, the shamrock decal — lit by a rig of its own
  (weapon3d "pbr": a key, a fill, a made-up sky for the metal to
  reflect) instead of the flat gun shader the other guns wear. A potato
  leaves down the sight, lofted, falls, and BOUNCES off walls, floors
  and ceilings until it touches somebody — or its eight-second fuse
  runs out, or it rolls to a stop; the shooter is safe from it for the
  first few tics. Behind it a thick trail of added light, a hue on each
  puff. The warhead: a nuke's worth of blast and fire, a white flash,
  and a fireball, a climbing column, a cap and a ring of shock, every
  colour and cycling (shaders/nuke.gdshader), a storm of rainbow sparks
  and the heaviest shake in the game. While a potato is in the air the
  palette gives way by half, and while a nuke burns all but completely
  (Lofi.set_unsnap), so the rainbow reaches the glass as a rainbow. Six
  in the hopper, one back every two seconds. godot/tests/potato_shot.gd
  takes its pictures; the weapons test fires it.

THE ANDROID BUILD (an APK)

  tools/build-android.sh [out.apk] (default build/android/mewd.apk):
  export_presets.cfg's "Android" preset — arm64, the Mobile renderer on
  Vulkan, landscape either way round, immersive, the network permission
  for a match — side-loadable on an Anbernic RG557 or a phone. Needs
  Godot 4.3 with its Android export templates, the Android SDK's build
  tools (export/android/android_sdk_path in the editor's settings) and
  Java. With MEWD_KEYSTORE, MEWD_KEY_ALIAS and MEWD_KEY_PASS it is a
  release build signed with that key; without, a debug build signed
  with the editor's debug key. Textures are imported ETC2/ASTC as well
  for it (project.godot).
  On GitHub, .github/workflows/android.yml builds it on every push to
  main or the Godot branch (tools/ci-android.sh sets up a clean runner)
  and puts it up as the rolling release "android". Repository secrets
  MEWD_KEYSTORE_B64, MEWD_KEY_ALIAS and MEWD_KEY_PASS give it a steady
  key; without them each build is signed with a new one. The runs share
  one queue whichever branch pushed (concurrency group android-release,
  never cancelled midway), so a push to both at once can no longer have
  one run delete the release under the other's upload; the Linux build
  (linux.yml, release "linux") does the same.

WHAT THE HANDHELD LAGGED ON, AND WHAT WAS DONE (at the user's request:

    the built area tiled, a big map's tiles bigger);
  - WHICH SECTOR A POINT IS IN (Level._in_sector) asked the whole outline
    every time, and a map in blocks makes the open ground one polygon
    round every block on it — the maze's 624 corners, THE SPRAWL's
    1,474 — while every person, piece and drop asks many times a tic:
    a big sector's edges are now kept in bands across it (Level._band)

MARKS THAT LIE ON THE GROUND: PROJECTED DECALS

  At the user's request ("true decals, so that impact effects look good
  and consistent"), every mark the game lays is now a projected decal,
  one way for all of them. That covers a hole, a hot hole, blood and a
  pool, gore's spatters, the flamer's hot spot and the scorch it leaves,
  the lance's sear and slag, a rocket's crater, the potato's glass and
  the arc gun's burn.

  WHAT THEY WERE. Two kinds. Quads were flat squares laid on the
  surface's normal and lifted a little off it. On the island's uneven
  ground a quad hung in the air over a dip, was cut by a hummock, and
  on a hillside laid flat at the hill's average slope. Real decals were
  Godot's Decal nodes, one per mark. The Mobile renderer draws at most
  eight decals on one mesh, and an island chunk is 128 m of hillside, so
  they were switched off when the islands came in (see THE ISLAND).

  WHAT THEY ARE (shaders/decal_project.gdshaderinc). Each mark is a box
  standing on the point it landed on. Its x and y are the mark's picture
  and its size; its z is the surface's normal. The box is drawn with
  its own shader, which reads the depth already drawn under each of its
  pixels and turns that depth back into a point in the world. It then
  takes that point into the box's own space and paints the mark there,
  or draws nothing if the point is outside the box. So the mark is
  painted on whatever surface is inside the box, as that surface lies.
  It follows a slope, wraps a hummock and stops where the ground drops
  away, and nothing hangs in the air.

  Some details:
  - The box is half above the surface and half under it. Its depth is a
    share of its width (THICK in render/decals.gd: a hole 1.0, blood and
    heat 0.6, a sear 0.5, a crater 0.45).
  - The last 40% of that depth either way fades out.
  - Paint also fades out where the surface turns away from the mark's
    normal, worked out from the depth per pixel. So a crater on the
    ground does not smear up a cliff, or up anyone standing in it.
  - The box is drawn from inside, with no depth test. Its back faces
    cover every pixel of it whether the eye is outside the box or
    standing in a crater. The depth read back decides what shows, so
    something in front of a mark still hides it.
  - A mark laid on the ground takes the terrain's own normal there
    (Level.normal_at), not straight up. Gore's surface basis is turned
    onto it the same way. The lifts off the surface are gone.

  WHAT IT COSTS. Nothing new to draw: every pool was one MultiMesh of
  quads and is now one MultiMesh of boxes (12 triangles a mark instead
  of 2). There is no limit per mesh, nor tiles, nor an atlas. The
  shaders are the same ones with a projection put in front; what each
  mark looks like and how it cools is unchanged.

  WHAT WENT. RealDecals (render/real_decals.gd), its count on the
  performance overlay, and its warm-up. In its place, Decals.warm_begin
  and GoreDecals.warm_begin lay one mark of every kind on the ground
  ahead during WARMING UP THE SHADERS, so every decal shader is compiled
  there and not at the first shot. warm_end takes them back.

  A PICTURE: godot/tests/mark_shot.gd lays one of everything on the
  ground ahead of you and looks at it (--nograss hides the plants,
  --under puts a crater at your feet). godot/tests/island_shot.gd takes
  a sixth argument, the pitch the gun is held at.

THE PICTURE: SQUARE PIXELS, AND ONLY WHOLE-NUMBER SCALES

  At the user's request ("square pixels at 480p, render at 720p, the
  whole canvas only responding to mathematically acceptable ratios that
  won't result in distortion"), the defaults are now square pixels
  (PIXEL ASPECT SQUARE) on a 480-row grid (PIXELS 480), with the world
  drawn at 720 rows (RENDER 720). The prefs' version went up (15), so a
  saved set from before is put back to these. Later, also at the user's
  request ("new default 960 render 480 pixel filter"), the world is drawn
  at 960 rows by default (RENDER 960, Lofi.RENDER and pause.gd
  DEFAULTS), still filtered onto the 480-row grid; the prefs' version is
  16, so saved prefs go back to the new defaults.

  NO FRACTIONAL SCALE, ANYWHERE (Lofi._resize). Every chunky pixel is a
  whole number of the screen's own pixels across and down, so no column
  or row is ever wider than its neighbours. The scale is worked out in
  PHYSICAL pixels, because the window is stretched as canvas items and
  the canvas's units are not the screen's. It is the whole number that
  brings the grid's rows nearest the PIXELS asked for:
    1080 screen   x2 = 540 rows
     720 screen   x2 = 360 rows
    1440 screen   x3 = 480 rows
  The columns are as many as fit at that scale. Anything left over, less
  than one chunky pixel each way, is a black border, centred. A
  non-square PIXEL ASPECT is likewise a whole number of screen pixels
  across, the nearest to the ratio asked for. The world's buffer is drawn
  at exactly the grid's aspect: RENDER rows, never more than the
  screen's own. The filter boxes it down onto the grid. Measured at 720
  and 1080: every chunky pixel is exactly 2 x 2 screen pixels.

ROUNDS TAKE BITES: PIECES AND A GREAT DEAL OF BLOOD

  At the user's request ("I want stuff like the minigun to blow holes in
  sprites, before destroying them ... with people and creatures, chunks
  taken out of sprites shooting copious campy amounts of blood
  particles, eventually leading to their death / explosion"); then,
  backing up, at the user's request again: NO HOLES in anything, the
  pieces and the blood and the gore kept.

  A ROUND IS A BITE (Actor.damage, "wounds" in States). A person, a
  trooper, a unicorn, a foal or a street lamp takes a fixed number of
  rounds to finish, whatever the gun:
    shoppers, townsfolk, candy girls  10
    SWAT                               8
    soldiers                          12
    unicorns                          14
    foals                              8
    street lamps                       8
  Each round takes its share of the health. Before this, one minigun
  round killed a person outright (24 to 48 against 12). The round that
  finishes a body blows it apart: the gib death, never the fall.

  EACH BITE (Game.wound, from where Game.hitscan saw the round go in):
    - THE PIECE that was there flies off the way the round was going: a
      ragged card cut from the sprite's own picture, run red at its

      cut in it (the holes table and the shader's cut-outs are gone).
    - BLOOD, campy: a gout along the round's way, a burst every way from

  A lamp throws its piece and a few sparks instead.

  THE END: a body blown apart throws pieces of its own picture as well
  as the gibs (Game.gib). A lamp, shot or blown up, breaks into pieces
  of itself and a puff, and is gone (Game.break_apart, "breaks").

  THE PIECES (render/sprite_chunks.gd, shaders/sprite_chunk.gdshader)
  are cards cut from a picture: a strip's cell or a plant's sprite.
  They tumble, bounce on the ground, come to rest and go. There is one
  MultiMesh per picture, so a tree in eighty pieces is one draw, and at
  most 1200 pieces in all; when it is full, the eighth of them with the
  least life left go at once (one walk, not one a piece).
  THEY GO THE WAY A PLANT GOES (at the user's request): fading away is
  the same ordered 4x4 Bayer screen door the plants' near fade dithers
  with, not a random speckle, and a PLANT'S piece also dithers out near
  the eye as the plants do (gone inside 1.5 m, whole past 4 m). A body's
  pieces do not near-fade.

THE PLANTS AND THE GRASS: SHREDDED

  At the user's request ("vegetation hit by explosives like the rocket
  blows up in chunks and stops existing"); then, backing up, at the
  user's request: NO FIRE and NO HOLES — plants shredded into pieces of
  themselves, the big ones into more and smaller pieces, and the weapons
  shredding the grass too. See game/veg_damage.gd.

  The island's trees, bushes and ferns are the plant scatter's
  (SCRIPT_veg_scatter.gd). VegDamage does not keep a plant list of its
  own, and does not change how the scatter places anything: an edit to
  that file would throw away every island's plant bake. It finds the
  rows near a point in the scatter's live tile buffers (indexed in 8 m
  buckets, built once per tile) and rewrites just those rows. A plant
  shredded has its row scaled to nothing; the rounds a plant has taken
  are counted in its row's custom .y, which nothing draws. Every change
  is also kept and put back if the tile is ever built again.

  Game.explode): the plants inside 45% of its reach are shredded into
  pieces of their own picture (SpriteChunks) with a puff, and are gone.

  square of the plant, 6 at least and 80 at most, so a fern goes to a
  handful, a tall tree to eighty, and every plant's pieces come out
  about the same small size. Nothing is set alight (the plant fire, its
  spread and the `veg_clock` global are gone; the shader's crash-site
  burn is untouched).

  A ROUND (Game.hitscan) that meets a tree or a bush before anything
  else goes into it (VegDamage.ray, the plants as upright cylinders
  walked bucket by bucket along the line). It throws a scrap of the
  plant and counts against it; about nine of them shred it.

  THE GRASS (SCRIPT_grass_scatter.gd `mow`): a blast that goes off near
  the ground mows the tufts inside 60% of its reach, and a round into
  the ground the tufts within 0.55 m of where it went in; each tuft cut
  (24 at most a time) is thrown as a few pieces of its own picture. The
  grass scatter keeps what has been cut as circles of ground (4000 at
  most), not as marks on rows, because its tiles are pruned and rebuilt

THE SPARKLE, THE TREES THAT WOULD NOT BLOW UP, THE GORE AS IT WAS

  At the user's request ("all sprites still have the sparkly weirdness
  around them, trees don't blow up into chunks, I want NPCs to blow up
  like they used to with the explosion and copious gore chunks and blood
  spatter, delete the blood cloud spray; fix the vegetation, make road
  side grass to next terrain splat smoother transition, make the hills
  around the towns even softer and less cliffy"; "smallest tree chunk
  should be 16x16 pixels, same with all other veg").

  THE SPARKLE round every sprite was two things, and the fix above
  (clear texels recoloured) reached neither:
    - THE STRIPS' EDGES WERE MATTED. The people's strips (shoppers,
      shoppers_hd, swat, army, and the blast, giblets and splat strips)
      were cut out by tools that left each half-clear texel of the edge
      the drawing's colour MIXED WITH BLACK: a texel 60% coat was 60% as
      bright as the coat. Drawn through a cut-out at half alpha, every
      one of those is a dark bead round the drawing. tools/
      fix-sprite-fringe.py gives every texel that is not fully opaque
      the colour of the nearest fully opaque one (alpha untouched), and
      the strips in assets/people are rewritten by it. (The candy strips
      — the girls, the unicorns, the lamp — have hard edges and needed
      nothing.)
    - A NEAREST FETCH OF A DRAWING FINER THAN THE PIXELS. The HD strips
      and the plants' paintings have several texels under each pixel
      once they are a few metres off; a nearest fetch picks one of
      them, a different one as the sprite sways, and at the cut-out's
      edge that is a rim of texels that come and go — a twinkle round
      everybody and every tree. standee.gdshader and
      SHADER_veg_billboard.gdshader now carry the picture twice, once
      nearest and once filtered (`strip_smooth`, `albedo_smooth`, bound
      to the same texture: Standees._strip, SCRIPT_grass_scatter, and
      for the plant scatter VegDamage.bind_smooth from the game, since
      the scatter's source is in every island's bake digest), and the
      fragment takes the filtered one by how many texels its pixel
      covers (the derivatives of UV): nearest at one to one and under,
      so pixel art up close is still pixel art, filtered from two up —
      a proper average of what is under the pixel, which the lo-fi grid
      pixelates anyway.

  THE TREES WERE NOT BLOWING UP because nothing in SpriteChunks was ever
  drawn: the "too small to see" test in sprite_chunk.gdshader measured a
  piece's height on the screen with PROJECTION_MATRIX[1][1], which is
  NEGATIVE under the Vulkan renderers (their clip space is upside down
  to GL's), so every piece was under three pixels and collapsed. abs()
  of it. And two more reasons a rocket into a tree left the tree:
    - A ROCKET FLEW THROUGH THE TREE and went off on the ground beyond
      it. MissileSystem._fly_tic now asks VegDamage.ray along each tic
      of flight (after its first two, so a rocket fired from inside a
      bush clears it) and goes off in the plant it hits.
    - THE BLAST REACHED TOO LITTLE: a plant was blown only when its
      trunk was inside 45% of the blast's reach (2.7 m of a rocket's
      5.9). 85% now: a tree five metres off goes.
  NO PIECE UNDER 16 x 16 TEXELS of its picture (SpriteChunks.TEXELS):
  burst coarsens its grid until every cell is at least that, and a
  round's scrap off a plant (VegDamage.shoot) is 16 to 24 texels of the
  picture drawn AT THE SIZE IT IS ON THE PLANT — it was drawn at a
  fixed 4 to 7 units whatever the tree's size, which was confetti.

  THE GORE AS IT WAS. A person who comes apart (Game.gib, from the GIB

THE SHAKE, AND PLANTS THAT TAKE A BEATING FIRST

  At the user's request ("when NPCs are hit, especially when the cerebral
  bore is drilling into them, their sprites vibrate violently: a small
  pulse per normal weapon hit, continuously during drill boring; the same
  with trees and bushes and objects: vibrate per hit a few times, a
  higher threshold as the object's size increases, prior to destruction,
  so not immediate destruction, builds to payoff").

  PEOPLE AND BEASTS: every blow but a flame's (the stream is continuous,
  not a blow) sets Actor.shake to SHAKE_HIT (7 tics). Actor.jolt(tic) is
  where the drawing is thrown that tic, SHAKE_AMP (3.5 units) at the start
  of a jolt, dying to nothing on its last tic, so they are drawn standing
  still again. A bore in a head throws them about for all of its 78 tics,
  from 3 units to 7 as the head is about to go. The jolt is a hash of the
  actor and the tic, so Standees (the row, written every tic while they
  shake) and BoreSystem.draw (the bore in the head) agree on it. A street
  lamp or a unicorn is an actor too, so it shakes the same way.

  PLANTS: VegDamage.hits_for(w, h) is how many rounds a plant takes:
  2 + 1.5 * sqrt(w * h), between 3 and 24 (a fern 4, a bush 5 or 6, a
  tall tree 12 to 21). Each round counts one of them against it (the
  row's custom .y, as before) and SHAKES it. Custom .z is the clock when
  it was hit (Game.clock: seconds of play, stopped while paused, handed
  to the shaders as the global shake_clock every frame), and .w is how
  hard: 7% of its width, between 0.1 m and 0.7 m, half that on the first
  round and half again as much on the last. SHADER_veg_billboard throws
  the quad about for 0.45 s, a new throw forty times a second, the top
  thrown furthest and dying away. So the shake costs one row write a hit
  (which the hit already made), not a write a frame. A rocket now blows
  apart everything within BLOW_SHARE (half) of its reach, about three
  metres. Out to SHAKE_SHARE (1.2) of it, plants are shaken and take up
  to BLAST_HITS (10) rounds' worth, falling to none at the edge. A fern
  at the inner edge still goes; a big tree is left shaking and hurt, and
  a second rocket finishes it. godot/tests/damage_test.gd checks it all:
  the jolt and the stillness after, a bore shaking her every tic, plants
  outside the blast shaken but standing, and a tree shaken on the first
  round.

THE PERFORMANCE LOG

  At the user's request: first "massive lag with too many decals; don't
  correct anything yet, but add verbose logging to a log file", then,
  with the first log read, "add logging for LITERALLY EVERYTHING with
  frame and cpu counts". Nothing about the game has changed for it.
  PerfLog (godot/scripts/core/perf_log.gd) writes what everything costs:
  - On a desktop it writes mewd-perf.log in the user's home folder
    (%USERPROFILE% on Windows).
  - On the handheld it writes to user://.
  - Each game writes it afresh and keeps the game before as
    mewd-perf.prev.log.

  Every line is stamped with the milliseconds since the game began and
  the frame. The lines:
  - FRAME, every frame:
    - the frame's wall time, the engine's whole _process and
      _physics_process time, the renderer's CPU and GPU time for the
      frame before, draw calls, primitives and objects;
    - sec: every section of Game._process and Game.tic in ms, each timed
      on the microsecond clock (Game._prof_add feeds the log): look,
      tics (and within them tic.players, tic.weather, tic.actors,
      tic.tracers, tic.decals, tic.streams, tic.guns, tic.escalation,
      tic.fx, tic.giblets, tic.chunks, tic.bleed, tic.veg_damage,
      tic.trophies, tic.rest), camera, draw.beam, touch+zoom, scopes,
      weather+light, forest_view, standees, draw.tracers, draw.streams,
      draw.guns, draw.escalation, draw.fx, draw.giblets, draw.chunks,
      draw.trophies, weapon3d; and isl.*: the island's own nodes
      (IslandWorld, VegScatter, GrassScatter, VegBurn, TimeOfDay,
      CloudSea, HorizonClouds), whose _process the log runs itself, after
      Game's, so each can be timed; other is what the engine's process
      total has that no section accounts for, log the log's own cost;
    - n: the counts — tics this frame, actors awake and asleep, sprite
      rows written, pieces, gibs, particles, missiles, bores, live marks
      by pool, heat spots, nodes, objects, orphans, static memory, video
      memory;
    - did: what happened this frame — hitscans, spawns, wounds, gibs,
      explosions, marks laid by kind, plant rows written, and whether the
      plant and grass scatters are owed a rescan and how many of their
      tiles are pending.
  - PLACE: every mark laid, with its pool and kind, the slot it took, the
    pool's live count after it, and where and how big it was. A full pool
    is noted, since the oldest mark is then overwritten.
  - SEC, once a second: fps, the worst frame, the marks laid, and every
    section's average over the second, biggest first.
  - SPIKE: any frame over 40 ms, with its three biggest sections and
    what was laid in it.
  - EVENT: what the BlackBox marks (a level, a gun change, a pause, a
    quit).

  Lines are buffered and flushed once a second. Headless runs (the tests)
  write nothing unless given --perf-log.

  WHAT THE FIRST LOG SAID (a Quadro M6000, 1920 x 1008, three and a half
  minutes of Candy Land): the GPU took 3 to 4 ms a frame nearly the whole
  run, the renderer's CPU under a millisecond, and every pool was full
  (hole 400, gore 480) from the first minute while the game held 60 fps —
  so the number of marks is not the lag. The spikes (40 to 90 ms) came
  with the minigun firing (holes laid, trees shot), with eight hundred
  plant pieces in the air, once at 215 ms on a gib, and at the end a
  stretch where the GPU climbed to 62 ms as rockets filled the blast pool
  and then five seconds at 7 fps with the GPU at 3 ms and nothing laid:
  script time, which the first log could not place and this one can.

THE BIG MARKS ARE BAKED

  At the user's request ("the more rocket impact decals in view, the
  lower the FPS"; "fix it"). The first performance log had said so: in
  its last stretch the GPU went from 4 ms a frame to 62 as the blast
  pool filled, and blast_decal.gdshader was why. A crater's picture —
  four octaves of noise several times over, the cracks, the rays, a
  26-step shrapnel loop — was drawn again EVERY FRAME for EVERY PIXEL of
  every crater's box, with no depth test to skip any of it (a projected
  decal cannot have one), so thirty craters in view ran the whole thing
  thirty times a pixel. The pool count never mattered; what was in front
  of the eye did.

  Now a mark's picture is drawn ONCE, when it is laid. Decals has a bake
  atlas: a 2D SubViewport (1024 x 320: the blast pool's 160 slots as 16
  tiles a row, each tile two 32 x 32 layers side by side) that is never
  cleared after its first draw. Decals._bake puts a ColorRect over the
  slot's tile with blast_bake.gdshader (the noise and the shapes exactly
  as they were, in a canvas shader with blending off so its channels
  are stored as numbers) and asks the viewport for one draw; the rect is
  put away the frame after. The mark's row carries its slot in custom y
  (its whole part; the seed in the fraction), and blast_decal.gdshader
  fetches the tile — two texture reads, nearest, which is the 32-texel
  LOW RES the marks already had — and does only what moves: the heat
  dying, the shockwave ring, the embers' flicker (one noise), the
  rainbow turning and the glints twinkling, the arc figure's crackle.
  What each layer holds is in blast_bake.gdshader's header. The viewport
  is HDR under Mobile (U.raw_out) so nothing is gamma-bent on the way
  through. The sears (sear_decal.gdshader, the lance's) still draw
  theirs every frame; they are the same kind of shader and the next to
  bake if the lance shows the same lag.

  at once at most, the oldest let go) and sets NOTHING alight — it is a
  picture of a fire where something was, not a fire. damage_test counts
  the pyres and that nothing burns.

  THE MICRO-QUEUES ("a slight delay on multiple vegetation and people
  explosions events ... to lessen cpu load"): yes, it makes sense — a
  rocket into a wood was forty bursts of pieces in one frame. Plants:
  VegDamage.blow_up bursts BLOW_PER_TIC (6) a tic and queues the rest
  (`tic` drains it, oldest first); a blast sorts its plants nearest
  first, so a wood goes up as a ripple outward. People: Game.explode
  sorts its victims by distance, the nearest BLAST_PER_TIC (4) take the
  blast now and the rest four a tic after (_blasts_tic). Nothing is
  lost: a queued plant that is already gone is skipped, a queued victim
  already dead is skipped. damage_test: the nearest at once, the rest a
  tic or two after; drop_test drains the queue before it looks.

  MARKS OFF THE SPRITES ("make sure decals don't affect billboard
  sprites"), two ways. Every decal material has render_priority -4
  (Decals.PRIORITY; the gore decals too): the marks, the people, the
  pieces and the particles are all transparent, and Godot draws
  transparent things by priority first and distance after, so a mark is
  drawn under whoever stands in it, never over them. And in
  decal_project.gdshaderinc, a pixel whose depth jumps more than 15% of
  itself to the next (DP_EDGE) — the rim of a plant or a person against
  the ground behind — is discarded outright: the normal read off such
  an edge was nonsense and could pass the facing test (the plants are
  opaque and in the depth buffer; the facing test already kept the
  ground's marks off their vertical faces).

  THE CEREBRAL BORE IN HAND ("i want to be able to see more of the
  cerebral bore"): held further out and a size up (Weapon3D GUNS: fit
  0.95 of GUN_LENGTH, out 2.3, a touch right and down), so the drum and
  the body are in the frame and not a corner of the drum. AND ITS LASER
  LEAVES THE GUN ("laser sight is not aligned to gun"): the line drawn
  from the sight (BoreSystem.draw) started at Game.nozzle, the fixed
  point beside the eye every shot flies from; now it starts at the held
  model's muzzle as drawn on the screen (Game.muzzle_view, what a
  round's streak already used). lance_shot has a `bore` mode for the

  shopper.

POSE TEMPLATES FOR AN IMAGE MODEL

  At the user's request ("a generic and adaptable sprite sheet with
  all conceivable contingencies — walking, running, shooting, crouch-
  crawling, dying — a super generic human pose visual metadata sprite
  sheet that, when paired with base character images and fed to an
  image model with an extremely explicit prompt, results in consistent
  Doom style character sheets"), and then ("extrapolate and do this for
  non combatants too"). tools/spritegen/, three parts and the pictures
  they make; its README.md is the manual.

  THE TEMPLATES (tools/spritegen/pose_sheet.py -> out/doom, out/mewd,
  out/civ, out/mewd-civ). A sheet is a grid of equal cells on flat
  magenta, and in every cell a grey mannequin in one pose from one
  angle: a stick figure with capsule limbs, its right arm and leg
  tinted red and its left blue, a dark patch for the face where the
  face shows, a dark bar for the gun, a yellow star for a muzzle
  flash, a brown box, bag, phone or broom for a civilian's prop, a
  compass in the band above it with the camera at the bottom and an
  arrow the way it faces. The band carries the cell's tag (A1, B5, H,
  RUN-2/3); a panel beside each row says the pose in words. The
  mannequin is drawn, not painted: a rig of fifteen joints in metres
  posed by angles (lean, twist, each shoulder's pitch and spread and
  inward swing, each elbow, each hip, each knee, and a root transform
  for the whole body: its height, its pitch and roll for lying down),
  turned about the vertical by the view's angle, projected from a
  camera ten degrees above level, depth-sorted and drawn far-first and
  a little darker. Every cycle is one function of phase at four
  frames; the gibs are the standing figure's parts thrown by seeded
  velocities and fallen under gravity to the floor. So the eight views
  of a pose come off one definition and the mirrored pairs really are
  mirrors. The rig is a touch stockier than life, as Doom's people
  are: the Zombieman sheet the user sent measures about six heads
  tall and well over half as wide as he is tall across the shoulders
  and arms, and his corpse as long as he stood and about half as
  high; the rig's head and limbs were thickened toward that, and the
  prompt's style section now gives those proportions in numbers
  instead of "a slightly big head".
  Two things found and fixed on the way to the civilians. The rig's
  lean had the sign the words did not mean: every "leaning forward"
  pose leaned back (the run was running backward from the waist up,
  plain in the profile views). The sign is flipped so positive is
  forward, and the poses tuned by eye against the old sign (prone,
  swim, knocked, fall) keep their look. And a raised arm's spread
  turns about the torso's forward axis, so for an arm pointing up a
  positive spread swings it INWARD: the hands-up, cheer, wave, flee
  and victory arms crossed over the head until their spread went
  negative.
  A figure now sits in its cell centred on its own extent, not its
  pelvis (a stride or a levelled rifle used to run past the cell
  edge), the tall cells are 0.72 of the cell height wide, the figure
  is a shade smaller (0.82 of the cell for 1.94 m), the ground line a
  little lower, a gib piece's spinning end stays above the floor, and
  the generator checks every cell's projected extent against its band
  and edges as it draws and reports any crossing: none in 994 cells.
  The views are Doom's and the troop strips': 1 head on, 2 a quarter
  turn toward the viewer's left, 3 side on facing left, 4 three
  quarters from behind, 5 from behind, and 6, 7, 8 the mirrors of 4,
  3, 2. out/mewd is the game's own four troop sheets with five views
  (walk A-D; aim, fire, pain E-G; death H-N; gibs O-W: exactly
  states.gd's TROOPS, prep-troops.mjs's eleven rows), 51 cells.
  out/doom is those with all eight views and thirteen more for the
  battle-royale direction, which have no states in the game yet: run,
  crouch and crouch walk, prone and prone crawl, knocked-down crawl,
  jump, melee, reload, throw, use, free fall, swim, victory; 402 cells
  on 17 sheets.
  THE NON-COMBATANTS are the same rig empty-handed or with a prop. The
  arm angles that put a hand somewhere (over the head, on a hip, at
  an ear, on a knee, on the ground) were solved numerically against
  the rig. out/mewd-civ is what a civilian strip would need, five
  views, letters A to Z: walk A-D (arms swinging), flee E-H (a panic
  run, arms up), stand I (at ease), cower J (crouched, hands over the
  head), hands up K; death L-R (the troops' death with empty hands),
  gibs S-Z (eight frames, the letters run out); 70 cells on five
  sheets. out/civ is those with eight views and fourteen more sheets:
  standing about (arms crossed, hands on hips, hands in pockets);
  flinch, kneel and cover, kneel with the hands behind the head; sit
  (on a chair that is not drawn, on the ground, cross-legged); talk,
  shrug, point; wave, clap, cheer; carry a box; on the phone and
  looking at one; sweep with a broom and kneel at work; pick up (a
  squat to the ground, then rising with a bag); limp (the right leg
  stiff, the left hand on the wound, blood); on fire (flailing, the
  flames left to the game as A_Torch draws them); dance; lie (on the
  back asleep, curled on the side, slumped against a wall that is not
  drawn); swim and free fall; 471 cells on 19 sheets. The civilian
  sheets are named civ-walk, civ-flee ... so the cutter never takes a
  civilian walk for a troop's, and their titles say CIVILIAN, which
  the prompt keys on. The game's own townsfolk are still one drawing
  each (SHOP, CANDY: see THE CANDY GIRLS); a civilian strip has no
  state table yet, and the cutter says so when it writes one.
  Beside each sheet a JSON manifest gives every cell's box as a
  fraction of the picture, its tag, animation, frame, view, whether it
  is in the air, and its words; and a prompt file. KEY.png is the
  legend (a troop aiming and a civilian carrying a box) and the eight
  views seen from above, to go with every request. The sheets are
  padded to an aspect an image model can return (1:1, 4:3, 16:9 ...)
  and saved as palette PNGs.

  THE PROMPT (tools/spritegen/PROMPT.md, and each sheet's .prompt.txt
  with that sheet's own contract appended: its grid, its columns'
  angles, its rows' poses cell by cell, its tags, and on a civilian
  sheet a block that says three ways there is no weapon anywhere and
  that the brown things are the props the rows name). Written long and
  repetitive on purpose: what each mark on the mannequin means, the
  grid contract the cutter depends on (same rows and columns, one
  figure a cell, nothing across a line, the bands untouched, flat
  #FF00FF behind every figure, no shadow, no ring, no arrow), the
  angle contract (what each of the eight views shows and the ways it
  goes wrong), the pose contract, the Doom style (a photographed clay
  model crushed to a palette at sixty pixels: hard edges, a dark
  outline, three to five flat tones a material, light from above and
  in front that does not turn with the figure, and the proportions
  measured off Doom's own sprites), the identity lock (one character
  in every cell, the same build, clothes, colours and gun or prop,
  asymmetry on the same side in every view), a list of don'ts and a
  checklist. A slot for the user's own notes on the character; a slot
  for a STYLE image (a sheet of original Doom sprites, such as the two
  the user sent, for the look and nothing else; they are not kept in
  the repository); a retouch line for one bad cell.

  THE CUTTER (tools/spritegen/cut_sheet.py NAME IMAGE...). Reads the
  model's output back off the manifest: each cell cut by its
  fractions (any return size), the magenta keyed by how magenta each
  pixel is and unmixed out of the halo as prep-troops does, specks
  dropped, the figure trimmed, and everything scaled by ONE factor
  (the manifest's pixels per metre against --height, 60 for a
  standing figure as the SWAT; 56 for a civilian, whose raised arms
  reach past a 64 cell at 60) so a corpse is as long as the man was
  tall; the alpha cut to one bit and the colour bled as candy-girls-
  strip does. Writes assets/people/<name>.png in the troop strip's
  shape — the turned letters in five views, then the flat letters,
  64 x 64 cells, the figure centred and standing on the bottom, an
  `air` frame kept at its height — taking the letters off the
  profile's own manifest, so a troop strip is A-G then H-W and a
  civilian's A-K then L-Z; and prints the TROOPS and _strip lines to
  add. A sheet name two profiles share is cut by the one the picture
  is the exact size of, else by --profile (mewd; a civ- sheet falls to
  mewd-civ), else by shape. Checked by cutting the templates
  themselves: the troops' 51 cells, 3264 x 64, the SWAT strip's size;
  the civilians' 70 cells, 4480 x 64, none empty.

  WHAT IT CANNOT PROMISE: the model's obedience. The README lists what
  goes wrong (a wrong angle in one cell, the character drifting
  between sheets, a moved grid, soft shading, scale drift, mirrored
  views that do not match, a weapon that creeps into a civilian's
  hands) and what to do about each; the five-view profiles, fewer
  cells a sheet, a STYLE image and a retouch of the one bad cell are
  the usual answers. Nothing here has been run against the image model
  itself.

THE LANCE'S CHARGE CRASH, SECOND LOOK: NO FONT ON THE PANEL

  At the user's report ("posi lance crashing again on charge (during
  mid charge)"), still only on the handheld (RG557, Mali) and still not

      every tic: the dirty key steps the charge ring in 40ths and the

    - THE BLACK BOX gets "lance: charging" and "lance: charge stage N"
      as each comes, so the next box says exactly how far in it was.
